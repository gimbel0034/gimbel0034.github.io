Personal & Holiday Safety Plan
Structured Sentences • Practical Steps

Core safety goals

I create a clear safety plan so that stressful moments feel more manageable and predictable. When holidays arrive—whether it is Halloween in Henderson or Christmas lights in Salt Lake City— I want my environment to feel safe, supportive, and grounded.

Emotional safety Physical safety Digital boundaries Community support

Step‑by‑step safety actions

  • I identify three safe people I can contact in Utah or Nevada when I feel overwhelmed.
  • I keep a short list of calming places, such as a quiet park in Salt Lake City or a sunny corner in Henderson.
  • I set a limit on social media during holidays so I do not compare my life to unrealistic images.
  • I prepare a grounding routine: slow breathing, naming five things I can see, and stepping outside for sunshine.

Emergency & support plan

  • I store local emergency numbers and crisis hotlines in my phone and on paper in my home.
  • I plan safe transportation for holiday events, avoiding driving when I feel exhausted or distracted.
  • I choose one trusted friend or family member to check in with me after major events like Halloween parties or Christmas gatherings.
  • I keep a small “comfort kit” nearby: water, a warm blanket, a favorite flower image, and a short list of reassuring sentences.

Psychology & Emotional Well‑Being Notes
Reflection • Coping Skills

Holiday emotions in simple sentences

I notice that holidays can bring mixed emotions. Sometimes I feel excited about pumpkins, lights, and sunshine; other times I feel lonely or overwhelmed. I remind myself that both experiences are valid.

  • I describe my feelings in one sentence: “Right now I feel anxious because there are many expectations around the holidays.”
  • I add a second sentence that offers kindness: “It makes sense that I feel this way, and I can take one small step to care for myself.”
  • I use a third sentence to plan an action: “I will step outside, notice the sun, and take three slow breaths.”

Psychology tools I can practice

  • I practice grounding by naming five things I can see, four things I can touch, three things I can hear, two things I can smell, and one thing I can taste.
  • I use cognitive reframing to gently challenge thoughts like “I must be perfect” and replace them with “I can be human and still be worthy.”
  • I schedule small joys, such as arranging flowers, carving pumpkins, or cooking a simple recipe.
  • I remember that seeking professional help is a strong and responsible choice when my emotions feel too heavy.

Simple recipes in clear sentences

I write each recipe as a short sequence of complete sentences. This helps me follow the steps calmly and safely, especially when I feel distracted.

Pumpkin Spice Oat Bowl
Prep: 10 minutes • Mood: cozy morning in Henderson
  • I cook oats in water or milk until they are soft and creamy.
  • I stir in pumpkin puree, cinnamon, and a small amount of sweetener.
  • I top the bowl with nuts, seeds, or a few dried cranberries.
  • I sit down, breathe slowly, and eat without rushing.
Warm Holiday Citrus Water
Prep: 5 minutes • Mood: winter evening in Salt Lake City
  • I heat water until it is warm but not boiling.
  • I add slices of orange or lemon and a small spoon of honey.
  • I hold the mug with both hands and feel the warmth.
  • I drink slowly while noticing the scent and taste.
Holidays, Recycling & Sunshine Habits
Halloween • Christmas • Eco‑Friendly

Holiday rhythm

I move through the year with a gentle rhythm: pumpkins in October, lights in December, flowers in spring, and sunshine in summer. Each season offers a chance to reset my habits.

  • On Halloween, I choose decorations that are reusable or recyclable.
  • On Christmas, I focus on meaningful connection rather than expensive gifts.
  • On sunny days, I step outside for a few minutes to feel the light on my skin.
  • All year, I sort my recycling and reduce waste where I reasonably can.
Pumpkins & costumes Lights & music Recycling bins Sunshine walks

I remind myself that small, consistent actions—like recycling, walking in the sun, and checking in with my feelings— can slowly change the way I experience holidays.

Citation & Study Reminder
Homework Support • Academic Style

Basic citation sentence

When I write homework or essays, I include a citation sentence to show where my information comes from. For example, I might write: “According to a recent article on holiday stress, many people feel pressure to be constantly cheerful.”

Sample in‑text citation (APA style)
In‑text sentence:
Holiday stress can increase when expectations are unrealistic (Author, Year).

I keep my citations organized in a small list at the end of my assignment. This habit supports academic honesty and helps me remember where I learned each idea.
